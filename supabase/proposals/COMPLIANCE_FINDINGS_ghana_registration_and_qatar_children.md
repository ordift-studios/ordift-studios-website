## A. Ghana Data Protection Commission registration: action plan
VERIFIED from the Act (Data Protection Commission's published text of Act 843) and the Commission's own pages:
- s.27(1): a data controller who intends to process personal data shall register. s.45(1): the Act applies to a controller established in Ghana processing data there, a controller using equipment or a processor in Ghana, or processing of information originating wholly or partly from Ghana; s.45(2): a controller not incorporated in Ghana shall register as an external company. s.53: processing without registration is prohibited; s.56: an offence (fine up to 250 penalty units or up to 2 years, or both).
- s.47: the application lists business name and address, description of the personal data and categories of people, whether special personal data is held, purpose, recipients, countries of transfer, classes of people, a general description of security measures, and anything else the Commission requires. s.50: renewal every two years. s.55: notify changes within 14 days. s.58: a certified data protection supervisor MAY be appointed.
- The Commission's pages: organisations that collect or process personal data in Ghana must register; start by creating an organisation account and company profile on app.dpc.gov.gh and submit a registration; fees are "based on sector classification" (the fee schedule was NOT published on the pages read); certificates are issued within 21 working days of approval and collected with a National ID (or an authorised representative with a letter); renewal needs a compliance report. The Commission has publicly said 2026 is a year of enforcement (press reports, not the Act).
- The public register (830 entries when checked) has no result for "Ordift", "Ordift Studios", "Tetey" or "Myredlive". The Commission's register page is NOT proof that no registration exists (a registration could be under another name, or older entries may not appear); the project records, Gmail and Drive also show none.
RECOMMENDATIONS (judgement, not law):
1. Treat Ordift Studios (Ghana) as a data controller that should register: it holds client, enquiry, instructor and website data of people in Ghana. No exemption in ss.60-74 (national security, crime, health, journalism/literature/art, domestic purposes, etc.) was identified that removes the registration duty for a commercial photography business; the journalism/literature/art exemption (s.64) is conditional and has not been analysed.
2. Whether the Qatar entity must also register (s.45(1)(c), s.45(2)) depends on whether it processes information originating from Ghana: unresolved.
3. Before applying: decide the registering entity name; collect the s.47 particulars (the personal-data inventory already drafted in the Privacy Notice and legal suite can seed it); confirm the fee category and amount with the Commission (needs your approval to contact them); decide whether to appoint a certified data protection supervisor (optional under s.58, but the renewal guidance references one).
4. Sequence: complete registration BEFORE activating Ghana-originating client permission requests (collecting names and evidence of permission is processing). The Privacy Notice contact change does not depend on it. After registering, use matetey@ordiftstudios.com as the contact so no amendment is needed.
5. If any registration is later found under another name or entity: s.55 requires notifying changes within 14 days.
NOT VERIFIED: the fee amounts and category thresholds; any additional documents the portal asks for; whether a foreign-held entity must register as an external company in practice (a law-firm note says the Commission does not insist, but that is secondary).

## B. Qatar photographs involving children
VERIFIED from the NCSA English translation of Law No. 13 of 2016 (not the official text; the Official Gazette no. 15 of 2016 is):
- Art. 16: personal data relating to children is "of a special nature" and may be processed only after obtaining the permission of the Competent Department, under the measures set by the Minister's decision. Art. 17: websites addressing children must give notice, obtain explicit consent from the child's guardian, give the guardian access and delete or suspend processing on the guardian's request. Art. 5(3) and Art. 10: erasure when the purpose ends; no longer than necessary.
SECONDARY (law-firm and compliance summaries; not verified against an official source): the Competent Department role is now with the NCSA (previously the Ministry of Transport and Communications); a "Special Nature Processing Request Form" exists; consent alone may not be enough (a permitted reason plus an additional condition); fines for breaching special-nature rules can reach QAR 5,000,000.
NOT ESTABLISHED: whether a published photograph of an identifiable child is "processing personal data of a special nature" needing a permit in practice, the current permit procedure, and whether contracted services (delivering photographs to a client) need one. Parental consent alone should therefore NOT be treated as sufficient.
INTERIM CONTROL (built, dormant until structured evidence is live): a photograph that shows an identifiable child and is marked as originating in Qatar cannot be approved or cleared for promotional portfolio use; internal handling for contracted services is unaffected. It depends on the candidate's jurisdiction field being set to Qatar, which is optional today: recommend making it required for any photograph showing a child.
NEXT STEP needing your approval: ask the NCSA National Data Privacy Office how it applies Art. 16 to promotional photographs of children and what the permit process is.

## C. Limitation periods checked for the retention window
- Ghana Limitation Act, 1972 (NRCD 54): periods run from 2 years (s.2: e.g. slander) through 3 years (s.3), 6 years (s.4, which court and practice sources say covers actions on simple contract) to 12 years (s.5, e.g. land); the section texts for ss.3-4 were only partly retrievable, so the exact categories need confirming.
- Qatar Civil Code (Law 22/2004), Art. 219 (verified in text): a claim for damages for an unlawful act is time-barred 3 years after the injured person knew of the damage and who caused it, or 15 years after the act, whichever is first; if the liability rests on a criminal act the claim does not time-bar while the criminal case is unresolved. The general contractual prescription (Arts. 403-418) was not retrievable.
CONSEQUENCE: a 12-month dispute window is shorter than every limitation period found (2-6 years in Ghana, 3 years in Qatar). It is a data-minimisation choice, not a defence-of-claims choice; see the schedule options in the report.
